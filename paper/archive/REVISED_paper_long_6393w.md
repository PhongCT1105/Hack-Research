## Abstract

**Background.** Large Language Models are increasingly used to draft personalized academic outreach messages, including emails to potential supervisors, mentors, and collaborators. In these settings the personalization often depends on describing a real professor's research. Fluent but unsupported claims about a recipient's topics, methods, findings, impact, or authorship can misrepresent their scholarly work.

**Methods.** We ran an offline, benchmark-style **pilot** evaluation of AI-generated professor outreach emails. A 2 × 2 factorial design varied whether the writer received a publication evidence packet during drafting and whether an evidence-based verifier revised the email after generation, yielding four conditions (A–D). We generated 96 emails for 12 professors across three fields and decomposed them into 360 atomic research claims. Claims were labelled against each professor's evidence packet by an automatic (LLM) evaluator. **All primary estimates in this paper are therefore pilot estimates from automatic labels, not human-verified measurements.** We quantified evaluator reliability against a blinded 36-claim human-labelled subset (10% of claims) and against a second automatic evaluator from a third model family.

**Results.** Under the primary evaluator, the closed-book baseline (A) had a Severe Error Rate (SER) of 0.344 (21/61 claims; exact 95% CI 0.227–0.477), with at least one severe error in 10 of the 22 emails carrying a judged factual claim. Grounding the writer in publication evidence (B) produced **no severe errors in 90 judged claims** (SER 0.000, exact 95% CI 0.000–0.040) while raising Supported Personalization Density (SPD) from 0.65 to 1.71 supported claims per 100 words. Verifying ungrounded drafts (C) yielded 1 severe error in 50 claims (0.020, 95% CI 0.001–0.106; SPD 1.04). The combined condition (D) also produced no severe errors (0/87, 95% CI 0.000–0.042) at the highest specificity (1.76). Grounding at write time outperformed post hoc verification on both axes.

**Interpretation and limits.** Agreement between the primary automatic evaluator and the human annotator was only **fair** (raw 55.6%, Cohen's κ = 0.33, 95% CI 0.16–0.49), below the conventional κ ≥ 0.6 threshold for treating a rater as reliable. Disagreement was one-directional in this subset: the evaluator was too lenient in 14 of 16 disagreements and never too strict, so the reported error rates are best read as lower bounds. Three caveats bound the result. First, the observed zeros are consistent with true severe-error rates up to about 4% at the claim level and about 14% at the email level, and are **not** evidence that grounding eliminates misrepresentation. Second, **the zeros are specific to the primary evaluator**: a second evaluator from a third model family, re-labelling the same claims, found 10 severe errors in condition B and 4 in D, with the closed-book rate at 0.455 — a large reduction under grounding, but not elimination. Third, 17 of the 21 closed-book severe errors came from the four psychology professors; excluding that field, the closed-book SER falls from 0.344 to 0.095. What holds under both evaluators, in every field, is that grounded conditions outperform both ungrounded ones and that grounding beats post hoc verification. The magnitude of the effect is not yet established.

**Conclusion.** In this pilot, giving an AI writer verified publication evidence sharply reduced severe misrepresentation of a professor's research — to zero under the primary evaluator and by roughly four-fold under the second — while making outreach emails more specific, and it did so more effectively than correcting drafts after generation. Grounding, rather than correction alone, is the more promising safeguard for recipient-focused academic outreach. Confirming the effect size requires human labelling at scale and a validated automatic judge.

**Keywords:** Large Language Models; Hallucination; Academic outreach; Grounded generation; Scientific claim verification; Personalized text generation; Factuality evaluation.

## 1. Introduction

Large Language Models (LLMs) are increasingly used as writing assistants for personalized text, including email-style messages and academic writing support. Prior work on personalized writing systems has shown that models can imitate or adapt writing style from very limited user data, while evaluation work has argued that personalized generation cannot be judged well by generic lexical metrics alone.<sup>1,2</sup>

Academic outreach is a distinctive case because personalization is not only about tone. A message to a professor often tries to demonstrate fit by referring to the recipient's research topics, publications, methods, datasets, findings, impact, or collaborations. This makes the task more sensitive than ordinary email polishing: a message may sound specific and respectful while still attributing the wrong method, overstating a result, inventing an impact claim, or combining unrelated papers into a misleading description.

The stakes are concrete. Prospective students, early-career researchers, and outreach tools increasingly rely on LLMs to draft messages at scale, and a single automated message that misattributes a method or invents a finding can embarrass the sender and misrepresent the recipient's scholarly record. Unlike ordinary summarization errors, these mistakes are directed at a named individual whose real publications provide an objective ground truth, which makes the task both higher-stakes and unusually well suited to systematic measurement.

However, limited work directly evaluates recipient-focused academic outreach emails that describe a specific professor's research, decomposes those descriptions into claim-level units, checks them against publication evidence, and measures whether safeguards preserve useful specificity rather than merely deleting risky details. This study addresses that application-specific gap with a controlled 2 × 2 pilot comparing evidence supplied during drafting against evidence-based post-generation verification.

The primary research question is: *how do publication grounding during drafting and evidence-based post-generation verification affect the factual accuracy of research-related claims in AI-generated outreach emails to professors?* The secondary objective is to measure the factuality–specificity trade-off — whether safer outputs remain genuinely personalized, or whether safety is achieved merely by deleting the specific details that give an email value.

This is a pilot study. Its contribution is a measurement framework, a reproducible pipeline, and a first effect estimate with explicitly reported uncertainty — not a settled effect size.

## 2. Related Work and Research Objective

### 2.1 Personalized text and email generation
Research on personalized text generation shows that language models can adapt wording, tone, ordering, and perceived relevance to a target reader or sender context. In email settings, Panza demonstrates that personalized email-writing assistants can learn convincing sender-style patterns from relatively small email collections and use retrieval-augmented generation to condition outputs on examples.<sup>1</sup> Organizational bulk-email research similarly shows that personalization affects engagement outcomes such as recognition, interest, and detailed reading.<sup>3</sup> However, these studies mainly evaluate style, relevance, engagement, or user experience rather than whether claims about a real recipient are factually supported. In contrast, this project focuses on recipient-grounded personalization: the email must describe a professor's actual research accurately, not merely sound tailored.

### 2.2 Automated personalized-text evaluation
AuPEL argues that personalized generation should be evaluated across dimensions such as personalization, quality, and relevance rather than only with generic surface metrics.<sup>2</sup> More recent work on personalization-induced hallucination shows that personalization can itself distort factual reasoning, creating a tension between being specific and staying correct.<sup>4</sup> These findings support measuring not only whether an outreach email sounds personalized, but also whether its specific research claims are supported by evidence.

### 2.3 Atomic factuality evaluation
Our project adopts a claim-level view of factuality. FActScore introduced a fine-grained approach for evaluating long-form generation by decomposing text into atomic factual claims and measuring the proportion supported by reliable sources.<sup>5</sup> This is directly relevant because a professor-outreach email may contain several research claims inside one fluent sentence — a topic claim, a method claim, a dataset claim, and an impact claim. Evaluating the whole email as simply correct or incorrect would hide these differences. We therefore adapt atomic factuality evaluation to research-related claims in academic outreach emails.

### 2.4 Scientific claim verification
SciFact established a benchmark where scientific claims are checked against paper abstracts and labelled according to whether the evidence supports, refutes, or fails to establish the claim.<sup>6</sup> SciFact-Open extends this to a larger, more realistic open-domain corpus, showing that verification becomes harder as retrieval scale increases and that abstract-only evidence can support only a limited version of some claims.<sup>7</sup> These findings motivate our evidence policy: professor evidence packets should include not only publication metadata and abstracts but also selected passages for focal papers when method-, result-, or scope-sensitive claims require more detail.

Despite the overlap, scientific claim-verification benchmarks differ from our setting. They usually evaluate prepared scientific claims rather than naturally generated outreach emails. In our study, claims arise inside persuasive, personalized messages, which creates additional risks: the model may overstate novelty, infer collaborations, combine unrelated papers, or turn tentative results into broad-impact claims.

### 2.5 Grounded generation and post-generation correction
RARR demonstrates a research-and-revise workflow in which model outputs are checked after generation and minimally revised to improve attribution while preserving the original text where possible.<sup>8</sup> FLEEK presents an end-to-end pipeline for claim extraction, evidence gathering, factuality judgment, and correction.<sup>9</sup> SciFix focuses specifically on correcting false scientific claims.<sup>10</sup> These systems motivate the verification side of our design. On the grounding side, AGREE shows that explicit grounding and adaptation can improve grounded response generation and citation quality compared with weaker prompting or post hoc citation approaches,<sup>11</sup> and ScholarCopilot shows that retrieval-integrated academic writing can improve citation and writing quality in scholarly contexts.<sup>12</sup> These studies suggest that providing evidence during drafting may be more effective than asking a model to repair unsupported claims after the fact. Our 2 × 2 design tests this comparison directly.

### 2.6 Remaining gap
Taken together, prior research provides strong foundations for our methodology: personalized generation motivates the outreach setting; FActScore motivates atomic claim decomposition; SciFact motivates evidence-based support labels; and RARR, FLEEK, SciFix, and AGREE motivate verification and grounding interventions. However, these strands usually study separate parts of the problem. Email-personalization work rarely evaluates factual accuracy about a real recipient's research; scientific verification work rarely evaluates naturally generated outreach messages; and grounded-generation work does not directly measure whether factual safeguards preserve useful professor-specific personalization. The remaining gap is recipient-focused factuality in AI-generated academic outreach.

## 3. Methods

### 3.1 Study design
This is an offline, benchmark-style pilot study. No generated emails were sent to professors. The design is a 2 × 2 factorial comparison with two binary factors: whether the email-writing model receives an evidence packet during drafting, and whether a separate evidence-based verifier revises the draft after generation (Table 1). The design isolates the main effect of grounding, the main effect of verification, and their interaction. The comparison between conditions B and C is especially important, since it tests whether evidence is more useful during generation or only during repair.

**Table 1. Experimental conditions.**

| Condition | Writer receives evidence | Post-generation verification | Purpose |
|---|---|---|---|
| A | No | No | Closed-book baseline |
| B | Yes | No | Effect of grounding during writing |
| C | No | Yes | Effect of correction after ungrounded writing |
| D | Yes | Yes | Combined safeguards |

### 3.2 Sampling frame and evidence packets
We sampled 12 professors across three academic fields — computer science/AI, psychology/cognitive science, and biomedicine/public health — four per field. All public reporting uses anonymous IDs (CS-01, PSY-01, BIO-01, …). Professors were drawn from OpenAlex<sup>13</sup> with ORCID identifiers, restricted to mid-visibility researchers to limit the chance that the model already knew highly famous researchers from pretraining.

Each evidence packet contains an official faculty-page or lab-profile research summary; eight representative papers with title, abstract, publication year, authors, venue, DOI, and source link; one or two selected passages for a focal paper; the retrieval date; and provenance notes. Abstracts were reconstructed from OpenAlex and normalized to remove residual markup.

The evidence packet is treated as the agreed evaluation source, not as a complete representation of a professor's entire career. A claim can therefore be *unsupported* relative to the packet without being false in the world. Claims that the packet is not designed to judge fairly are labelled *outside evidence scope* rather than forced into the unsupported category. These are provisional pilot packets; full multi-source identity verification is future work.

### 3.3 Generation and verification
For each professor and condition, the pipeline produced two independent email drafts, giving 12 × 4 × 2 = 96 emails. The writer was OpenAI gpt-4o-mini (temperature 0.7, 220-word limit); the same prompt was used in all conditions, differing only by the presence of the evidence block.

In conditions C and D, a separate verifier (Anthropic claude-haiku-4.5, temperature 0) received the draft email and the evidence packet. The verifier followed a minimal-edit philosophy: it extracted research-related claims, checked them against the packet, preserved supported content, softened overstatements, and removed or generalized unsupported or contradicted content. Every deletion and rewrite was logged separately from the original draft, so the analysis can test whether factuality improved by preserving supported specificity or by erasing risky content. Models were accessed via OpenRouter, and every run recorded model, provider, prompt version, seed, and temperature.

### 3.4 Claim extraction and automatic evaluation
Each email was decomposed into atomic research claims — the smallest independently checkable statements about the professor's research — by a claim extractor (Anthropic claude-3-haiku). Extraction and labelling are separate steps with separate prompts. Every claim was then labelled against the evidence packet by an automatic evaluator (also Anthropic claude-3-haiku), which assigns one of the seven labels in Table 2.

**Table 2. Claim labels used for annotation.**

| Label | Meaning | Use in metrics |
|---|---|---|
| Supported | The evidence packet directly supports the claim as written | Counts toward supported specificity |
| Partially supported | The core idea is related, but the wording is broader or less precise than the evidence | Secondary factuality analysis |
| Overstated | The claim is directionally related but stronger than the evidence permits | Not reported (see §3.6) |
| Unsupported | The packet contains no support for a claim the packet should be able to support if justified | Counts as a severe error |
| Contradicted | The packet directly conflicts with the claim | Counts as a severe error |
| Outside evidence scope | The packet is not designed to judge the claim fairly | Excluded from the factual denominator |
| Subjective/generic | Praise or generic wording without a checkable research fact | Excluded from the factual denominator |

The writer (OpenAI) and the evaluator (Anthropic) are from different model families, so the evaluator does not grade text produced by its own family. **This separation does not hold for conditions C and D**, where the verifier (claude-haiku-4.5) and the evaluator (claude-3-haiku) are both Anthropic models; §5.3 treats this as a limitation. Condition B, which carries the study's central claim, involves no verifier and is unaffected.

### 3.5 Reliability assessment
Because automatic labels require validation, we assessed evaluator reliability two ways.

First, a human annotator labelled a blinded 36-claim subset (10% of all claims) spanning all four conditions and all three fields, working from a name-scrubbed evidence reference and blind to condition and to whether the text was an original or a verified draft. Human labels are treated as gold. We report raw agreement, Cohen's κ with a 95% confidence interval, the full human × machine confusion matrix, and per-condition coverage.

Second, all 360 claims were independently re-labelled by a second automatic evaluator from a third model family (gemini-2.5-flash-lite) to measure inter-evaluator stability.

Where the human and an automatic evaluator disagree, the human label governs. The human subset is small and unevenly distributed across conditions; §4.2 reports that distribution explicitly rather than aggregating over it.

### 3.6 Metrics and statistical analysis
The primary factuality metric is the **Severe Error Rate (SER)** = (unsupported + contradicted) / judged factual claims. The primary specificity metric is **Supported Personalization Density (SPD)** = supported research-specific claims per 100 words. Secondary measures are the email-level any-severe-error rate and verifier deletion/rewrite frequencies.

We had pre-specified an *overstatement rate* as a further secondary measure. We do not report it: neither automatic evaluator ever emitted the *overstated* label, on any of the 360 claims, so the metric is identically zero by construction rather than by finding. The human annotator did use the label. We treat this as a defect of the automatic instrument and report it in §4.2 rather than presenting a structurally empty metric.

**Confidence intervals.** Claims are nested within emails, which are nested within professors, so claims are not independent. We report two interval types and both are needed:

- **Exact (Clopper–Pearson) intervals** on the pooled claim counts. These are the primary report because they remain well defined when a condition contains zero severe errors.
- **A cluster bootstrap** resampling whole emails, which respects the nesting.

The cluster bootstrap is uninformative for a zero-event condition: if no email contains a severe error, every resample returns zero and the interval collapses to [0, 0]. A collapsed interval is an artifact of the resampling procedure, not evidence that the underlying rate is zero, and reporting it as a confidence interval would assert a certainty the data cannot support. For conditions B and D we therefore report the exact upper bound at the claim level and, because clustering means the effective sample size lies between the number of claims and the number of emails, also the exact upper bound at the email level. The true bound lies between the two.

A logistic mixed-effects model with professor and email random effects was considered but is not estimable at this sample size, particularly for the zero-event conditions; fitting it is deferred to an expanded dataset.

This research used only publicly available data and involved no clinical, wet-lab, or human-subject experimentation.

### 3.7 Anonymization, blinding, and reproducibility
Because ungrounded outputs can contain fabricated claims about real people, no real professor name appears in any released artifact. Each professor is referred to by an anonymous identifier, and the real-name-to-identifier mapping is kept in a private, non-released file. Generated emails, extracted claims, and the annotation sheet are stripped of names; annotators work from a name-scrubbed evidence reference and cannot see the condition or whether a text is an original or verified draft. All code, prompts, generated emails, labels, and figures are released so that the pipeline can be re-run.

## 4. Results

### 4.1 Primary pilot estimates

We generated 96 emails across 12 professors, four conditions, and two seeds per condition. The pipeline produced 360 atomic claims. The automatic label distribution was 246 supported, 62 outside evidence scope, 21 unsupported, 20 partially supported, 10 subjective/generic, and 1 contradicted, giving a factual denominator of 288 judged claims and 22 severe errors in total. **All estimates in this subsection are automatic pilot labels; §4.2 quantifies how far they can be trusted.**

Table 3 reports per-condition metrics. Two reporting details differ from a naive summary and matter for interpretation. First, the email-level denominator is the number of emails carrying at least one judged factual claim, not the number generated: in condition A, 2 of the 24 emails produced only outside-scope or subjective/generic claims and are therefore excluded from the email-level rate, giving 10/22 rather than a rate over 24. Second, conditions B and D contain no severe errors, so their intervals are upper bounds only.

The closed-book baseline (A) had a SER of 0.344, with at least one severe error in 10 of 22 judged emails. ### 4.2 Evaluator reliability and the uncertainty it adds

The estimates in §4.1 come from an LLM judge. This subsection reports how well that judge tracks human judgment (Table 4), and it is placed here rather than in the Discussion because it bounds every number above.

**Table 4. Agreement between annotators.** κ intervals are Wald intervals on Cohen's κ.

| Comparison | Claims | Raw agreement | Cohen's κ | 95% CI | Interpretation |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Primary evaluator (claude-3-haiku) vs human | 36 | 0.556 | **0.33** | 0.16–0.49 | fair |
| Primary evaluator vs second evaluator (gemini-2.5-flash-lite) | 360 | 0.725 | 0.50 | 0.44–0.57 | moderate |
| Second evaluator vs human | 36 | 0.694 | **0.54** | 0.35–0.74 | moderate |

Four points follow, and none of them is reassuring.

First, **the primary evaluator's agreement with the human annotator is only fair**, and the entire 95% interval (0.16–0.49) lies below the κ ≥ 0.6 threshold conventionally required before a rater is treated as reliable. The primary labels are not a validated instrument.

Second, **the second automatic evaluator agreed with the human better than the primary evaluator did** (κ = 0.54 versus 0.33). The choice of primary judge was therefore not the best available choice, and the primary estimates are drawn from the weaker of the two automatic raters. Re-running the full corpus with a stronger judge is a priority for the expanded study.

Third, and most consequentially for how the headline result should be read, **the zeros in conditions B and D are specific to the primary evaluator.** Running the second evaluator over the same 360 claims does not reproduce them (Table 5).

**Table 5. Per-condition Severe Error Rate under each automatic evaluator.** Both evaluators labelled all 360 claims independently; the factual denominators differ because the evaluators assign the excluded categories differently. Intervals are exact 95% intervals.

| Evaluator | A (closed-book) | B (grounded) | C (verify-only) | D (grounded + verified) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Primary (claude-3-haiku) | 21/61 = 0.344 (0.227–0.477) | **0/90 = 0.000** (0.000–0.040) | 1/50 = 0.020 (0.001–0.106) | **0/87 = 0.000** (0.000–0.042) |
| Second (gemini-2.5-flash-lite) | 35/77 = 0.455 (0.341–0.572) | **10/94 = 0.106** (0.052–0.187) | 11/54 = 0.204 (0.106–0.335) | **4/90 = 0.044** (0.012–0.110) |

The second evaluator finds **10 severe errors in condition B** and 4 in condition D, where the primary evaluator found none. What survives across both evaluators is the structure of the result, not the zero:

- The closed-book baseline is the worst condition under both.
- Both grounded conditions beat both ungrounded ones under both evaluators.
- **B < C — the study's key comparison — holds under both** (primary 0.000 vs 0.020; second 0.106 vs 0.204).

What does not survive is the claim that grounding eliminated severe errors, and the ordering of B against D (the second evaluator ranks D best, the primary ranks B best). We therefore treat the grounding effect as a **large reduction** in severe misrepresentation whose magnitude is judge-dependent — a reduction of roughly 4-fold on the second evaluator's labels — rather than as elimination. This is the single strongest reason not to read the zeros in Table 3 as a property of the emails.

Of the 16 disagreements, the automatic evaluator was **too lenient in 14, too strict in 0**, and orthogonally different in 2. In this subset the disagreement is genuinely one-directional, which is why the automatic rates are lower bounds. But the mechanism is more damaging than simple leniency:

- **The judge does not mislabel severe claims as supported; it moves them out of the denominator.** Seven claims the human called *unsupported* (6) or *contradicted* (1) were labelled *outside evidence scope* by the judge — a category explicitly excluded from the SER denominator. The judge therefore biases the denominator, not only the numerator, and SER is not simply an attenuated version of the true rate.
- **SPD is also inflated.** Six claims the human called *subjective/generic* were labelled *supported*. Of the 24 claims the judge called supported, only 16 were human-supported: a precision of 0.67 (95% CI 0.47–0.83). The reported SPD numerator is therefore inflated by roughly one third. Applying that factor uniformly gives approximate human-adjusted densities of 0.43 (A), 1.14 (B), 0.69 (C), and 1.17 (D) — the ordering across conditions is preserved, but the absolute values are not.
- **The *overstated* label was never emitted**, by either automatic evaluator, on any of the 360 claims, although the human annotator used it. One pre-specified secondary metric is thus unmeasurable with this instrument (§3.6).

Human-labelled coverage is also thin and very unevenly spread, which limits what the human subset can confirm (Table 7).

But conditions C and D rest on three human-labelled factual claims each — condition D on three claims from a single email — and their human intervals extend to 0.71. **The human subset confirms the closed-book baseline is bad and is consistent with grounding being better; it does not confirm the per-condition estimates for B, C, or D, and we do not claim that it does.**

### 4.3 The closed-book effect is concentrated in one field

Pooling across fields conceals a large heterogeneity that bears directly on how far the headline number generalizes (Table 8).

|---|---|---|---|---|
| Psychology | **17/19 = 0.89** | 0/30 = 0.00 | 0/15 = 0.00 | 0/31 = 0.00 |

| Biomedicine | 1/19 = 0.05 | 0/27 = 0.00 | 0/15 = 0.00 | 0/30 = 0.00 |

The pooled closed-book rate of 0.344 is not a homogeneous effect. **The four psychology professors account for 17 of the 21 closed-book severe errors (81%).** Per professor, two professors alone account for 11 of the 21, and 5 of the 12 professors produced no severe errors at all even closed-book. Excluding psychology, the closed-book SER falls to 0.095 (4/42, 95% CI 0.027–0.226); excluding computer science it is 0.474, and excluding biomedicine 0.476.

Figure 3 shows the per-field rates against the pooled estimate. It remains true that every field contained at least one unsupported or fabricated closed-book claim, and that no field produced any severe error under grounding. The *direction* of the grounding effect is consistent across fields. Its *magnitude*, however, is dominated by one field of four professors and should not be read as a general closed-book hallucination rate for academic outreach.

### 4.4 Verifier edit behaviour

The verifier's edit log distinguishes factuality achieved by preserving supported content from factuality achieved by deletion (Table 9).

**Table 9. Verifier edit decisions.** Intervals are exact 95% intervals.

| Condition | Edit decisions | Delete | Soften/rewrite | Keep | Deletion rate | 95% CI |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| C — verify-only | 49 | 27 | 14 | 8 | 0.551 | 0.402–0.693 |
| D — grounded + verified | 56 | 14 | 14 | 28 | 0.250 | 0.144–0.384 |

The verifier deleted 55.1% of edit decisions when repairing ungrounded drafts (C) but only 25.0% when the draft was already grounded (D). The difference is unlikely to be chance (Fisher's exact test, p = 0.0025). Correspondingly, the verifier kept 28 of 56 decisions in D but only 8 of 49 in C.

## 5. Discussion

### 5.1 Principal findings
In this pilot, grounding at write time (B) cut severe errors sharply while raising supported specificity relative to the closed-book baseline: factuality and usefulness moved together rather than trading off. This is the study's central observation. Absent evidence, a capable model readily produced plausible-sounding but unsupported research descriptions for a real professor, at a rate of 0.344 under the primary evaluator, 0.455 under the second, and 0.636 on the human-labelled subset. Placing the professor's real publications in front of the model before it wrote reduced that rate to zero under the primary evaluator and to 0.106 under the second, while making the emails more specific, not less.

Three boundaries on that statement should be stated plainly. First, zero observed errors in 90 claims is not a demonstrated zero: the data are consistent with a true severe-error rate up to roughly 4% at the claim level and roughly 14% at the email level. Grounding **reduced misrepresentation below the detection threshold of this instrument at this sample size**; it is not shown to eliminate it, and a deployment claim of "no misrepresentation" would not be supported by this evidence.

Second, and more pointedly, the zero is a property of the primary evaluator rather than of the emails. A second evaluator from a third model family, labelling the same claims, found 10 severe errors in condition B and 4 in condition D (§4.2). Read through that evaluator, grounding cut the severe-error rate from 0.455 to 0.106 — a large and useful reduction, and still the best-performing intervention, but plainly not elimination. The defensible form of the central finding is therefore that **grounding at write time produced a large reduction in severe misrepresentation while increasing specificity, with the reduction's size dependent on the judge and its floor not established.**

Third, the magnitude of the baseline problem is concentrated in one of three fields (§4.3), so the size of the improvement is far less well established than its direction.

### 5.2 Grounding versus correction
The key comparison, B versus C, favours grounding on both axes (B: SER 0.000, upper bound 0.040, SPD 1.71; C: SER 0.020, SPD 1.04). Evidence appears more valuable during writing than as after-the-fact repair, and the mechanism is visible in the verifier's edit behaviour: the deletion rate when repairing an ungrounded draft was more than double that when the draft was already grounded (0.551 versus 0.250, p = 0.0025). When a verifier must fix an ungrounded draft, it achieves factuality partly by removing risky but specific content, which lowers specificity; when the draft was grounded to begin with, the verifier finds less to delete and specificity is preserved. This answers the secondary question: the factuality–specificity trade-off is real for correction-after, and grounding largely avoids it.

This is the one part of the result that does not depend on which judge is believed. On the primary evaluator the factuality comparison is thin, resting on a single severe error in condition C, so the ranking of B over C would rest mainly on the specificity gap and the deletion-rate mechanism. The second evaluator separates the two conditions much more clearly — 0.106 for B against 0.204 for C, on 94 and 54 judged claims respectively — and ranks both grounded conditions above verify-only. **Grounding beats post hoc repair under both evaluators, on both axes, and in every field.** Of the study's claims, this is the best supported.

These results extend grounded-generation findings that supplying evidence during drafting outperforms post hoc citation<sup>11</sup> into a recipient-focused outreach setting with an explicit specificity measure, and they operationalize the personalization-induced-hallucination concern<sup>4</sup> in a concrete application.

### 5.3 Threats to validity

**The primary instrument is not validated.** Agreement with the human annotator was fair at best (κ = 0.33, 95% CI 0.16–0.49). We report the primary estimates as pilot estimates for that reason. The disagreement was one-directional in the validated subset — the judge was lenient in 14 of 16 disagreements and strict in none — which is why we describe the automatic rates as lower bounds. But two structural defects mean the bias is not a simple downward scaling: severe claims were reclassified into a category excluded from the denominator (7 of 36 claims), and generic praise was counted as supported (6 of 36), inflating SPD by about one third. The second automatic evaluator agreed with the human better than the primary one did, indicating the primary judge was a poor choice rather than an unavoidable limitation.

**Verifier and evaluator share a model family.** The writer (OpenAI) and evaluator (Anthropic) are deliberately from different families, but in conditions C and D the verifier (claude-haiku-4.5) and the evaluator (claude-3-haiku) are both Anthropic models. The near-zero rates in C and D may therefore partly reflect family agreement between the model that edited the text and the model that graded it. Condition B — which carries the central grounding claim — involves no verifier and is not exposed to this confound, and the second evaluator, from a third model family, still ranks both grounded conditions above both ungrounded ones (§4.2). But the C and D estimates specifically should be treated as more fragile than B's, and the second evaluator's higher rate for C (0.204) than for either grounded condition is consistent with post hoc repair being the weaker safeguard rather than with a family artifact alone.

**The headline zeros are judge-dependent.** The most serious constraint on this study is that its most quotable number does not replicate across instruments. The second evaluator, on the same claims, reports 0.106 for condition B and 0.044 for D rather than zero (§4.2, Table 5). Absolute rates in this paper should therefore be treated as instrument-relative throughout, and only the between-condition comparisons — which agree across both evaluators — as findings about the emails.

**Effect concentrated in one field.** As reported in §4.3, 81% of closed-book severe errors came from four psychology professors, and five of twelve professors produced none. The pooled baseline rate is not a general rate.

**Zero-event conditions.** Under the primary evaluator, two of four conditions contain no events, so the design has little power to distinguish B from D or to rank the grounded conditions against each other. The finding that D offers no factuality gain over B is a null result at this sample size, not evidence of equivalence — and the second evaluator in fact ranks D slightly ahead of B, which is a further reason to treat that ordering as undetermined.

### 5.4 Practical implications
For anyone deploying LLMs to draft academic or professional outreach, the actionable reading of this pilot is that retrieval grounding is the more promising default of the two safeguards: it was the only intervention that both reduced misrepresentation on every measure we applied and improved usefulness at the same time. Post-hoc verification remains valuable as a second line of defence — it reduced errors substantially on ungrounded drafts — but on its own it trades away specificity, and under both evaluators it underperformed grounding.

Two cautions follow for practice. First, these are reasons to prefer grounding in system design, not grounds for advertising a factuality guarantee to end users: a deployed outreach assistant should expect residual misrepresentation even when grounded, at a rate this study bounds only loosely (below roughly 4% on the primary evaluator's labels, near 11% on the second's). Second, our own experience is a caution about evaluation practice as much as about generation: had we relied on a single automatic judge, as many pipelines do, we would have reported elimination of an error mode that a second judge still detects. Teams evaluating factuality in deployment should run at least two independent judges before believing a zero.

### 5.5 Limitations and future work
Beyond §5.3: human validation covers 36 of 360 claims (10%), with only three judged factual claims each in conditions C and D, so per-condition human estimates are not available. Scaling human annotation is the single highest-value next step, and we would prioritise a stratified expansion — every claim the automatic evaluator flagged severe, plus roughly 30 claims per condition — which would yield per-condition human rates with usable intervals at a tractable annotation cost. We used a single writer model; other models may behave differently. Evidence packets are provisional and rest on abstracts plus abstract-excerpt passages, so a claim true only in a paper's full text would count against the model, again making SER conservative. The sample is small (12 professors, English only) and mid-visibility by construction, so results may not transfer to very famous researchers the model may have memorised. Future work should scale human labelling, adopt a validated or stronger automatic judge, use an evaluator family distinct from both writer and verifier, add cross-model writers, incorporate full-text evidence, and broaden the field and language coverage.

## 6. Conclusion

In an offline pilot benchmark of AI-generated professor outreach emails, giving the writer verified publication evidence sharply reduced severe misrepresentation of professors' research while making the emails more specific, and it outperformed post-generation verification on both counts. Grounding, rather than correction alone, is the more promising safeguard for recipient-focused academic outreach.

The direction of this effect is robust: it holds in all three sampled fields and under both independent automatic evaluators, and the comparison that motivated the study — grounding during drafting versus repair afterwards — favours grounding under both. Its magnitude is not established. The primary labels come from an automatic evaluator with only fair human agreement (κ = 0.33); the zeros it reports for the grounded conditions are not reproduced by a second evaluator, which finds a residual rate near 0.11; and the closed-book severity that defines the size of the improvement is concentrated in one of three sampled fields. Human labelling at scale, a validated automatic judge drawn from a family distinct from both writer and verifier, and a larger professor sample are the immediate next steps.
